ABSTRACT
In the electricity bidding market, power generation companies and distribution-oriented grid enterprises face
increasinglycomplexanddynamicdecision-makingchallengesthatcannotbefullycapturedbystaticorone-shot
approaches. To address these challenges, this study proposes a novel evolutionary game-theoretic framework
featuring a “mixed-strategy distortion” mechanism, which introduces adaptive periodic adjustments in bidding
ranges. This extension to classical evolutionary models reveals how cyclical constraints and strategic disruptions
influence the emergence of cooperative equilibria and market dynamics. The study develops a theoretical
framework for analyzing long-term stable equilibria in asymmetric evolutionary games involving distinct groups
ofpowergenerators,underpinnedbyarenewableenergygrid-connectedbenefitcoordinationmodel.Theoretical
analyses are validated through dynamic simulations that examine the interplay of key parameters, including
generation costs,local market demands,bidding ranges, andproduction capacities. Theresults demonstrate how
variations in these factors influence the evolution of bidding strategies, providing insights into the stability of
competitive equilibria in electricity markets. Furthermore, the findings highlight that rational adjustments to
bidding intervals can mitigate distortions, reduce electricity prices, and promote dynamic market stability. By
advancing the understanding of adaptive bidding strategies in competitive electricity markets, this research
offers actionable insights for policy development, market regulation, and strategic decision-making for power
generation enterprises and grid operators. These contributions provide a foundation for the design of more
efficient market mechanisms, promoting both economic efficiency and sustainable energy transitions.

2.1. Evolutionary game theory (EGT)
As mentioned earlier, traditional optimization and classical game
theory are often ill-suited for practical decision-making situations in
multi-subject power bidding, mainly because they assume a single or a
limited number of centralized objectives, perfect rationality, and com-
plete information among participants [8,9]. By contrast, EGT based on
theassumptionsof “boundedrationality”and “limitedinformation”has
gradually become a key mathematical tool for tackling complex multi-
agent games involving imperfectly rational individuals, as shown in
Table 1. EGT is generally considered to have been formally established
by Maynard Smith in 1973 [11], and its development originated from
researchinevolutionarybiology.Comparedtoone-shotgames—bethey
symmetricorasymmetric,cooperativeornon-cooperative—EGTfocuses
on populations rather than isolated players and emphasizes repeated
interactions, path-dependence, and dynamic stability. For instance,
symmetric or asymmetric Nash games typically assume a single equi-
librium point derived from static best-response conditions, whereas
evolutionary games offer a mechanism to explore how populations
converge (or fail to converge) to stable strategies over time through
imitation and adaptation. In their recent survey, Huang et al. (2022)
[29] outline three broad dimensions of game-theoretic applications in
electricity trading—cooperative, non-cooperative, and evolutionary
approaches—underscoring the complexity of modern markets where
intermittent renewables, flexible loads, and diverse ownership struc-
tures coexist. According to Ref. [29], the evolutionary route is particu-
larlypromisingasitenablesmodelingboundedrationalityanditerative
strategy learning. Building on these insights, our study delves deeper
into how such iterative learning processes respond to exogenously
triggered “mixed-strategy distortions,” thereby offering an additional
mechanismforunderstandinghowrationalorboundedlyrationalactors
adapt under cyclical market constraints. In this paper, we map these
biologicallyinspirednotionsofpopulation-levelstrategyevolutiononto
the electricity bidding context by considering two main stakeholder
“species”:renewableenergygenerationcompaniesandgridenterprises.
Moreover, we allow for bounded rationality and partial information,
reflectingreal-worldconstraintswhereeachpartyupdatesbidsbasedon
observed profitability rather than perfect foresight or instantaneous
optimization.

2.2. Research on the bidding behavior of power generators
In fact, some scholars have conducted relevant research on the bid-

bidding behavior analysis method based on game theory as examples,
theformerismainlystudiedaccordingtothefollowingtrainofthought.

3.2.2. Market clearing mechanisms
The first mechanism is unified market clearing price settlement
mechanism, that is, Market Clearing Price (MCP). This mechanism fol-
lows the principle of fairness. Under certain constraints, the trading
center first predicts the electricity demand for the next day in advance.
Then, power generation companies declare their own electricity gener-
ation andcorresponding electricitypricesto the tradingdispatchcenter
basedontheirownsituation.Subsequently,thedispatchcentersortsthe
declared electricity prices from low to high and allocates power gener-
ation loads to each power generation company, taking the bid price of
the power generation company that finally meets the next day’s elec-
tricitydemandasthesystemmarginalprice.UndertheMCPmechanism,
all power generation companies that successfully bid settle electricity
prices based on the system marginal price during the trading process.
The MCP mechanism is implemented based on economics, which has a
considerable theoretical foundation, and can provide some economic
theoretical guidance for achieving optimal resource allocation. Under
this mechanism, power generation companies prioritize successful bid-
ding, so they tend to declare low prices, allowing power purchasers to
buy electricity at lower costs [56]. However, under this mechanism,
theremaybeanegativecorrelationbetween theprofitmarginofpower
generation enterprises and the utilization rate of system resources. This
isbecausepowergenerationenterprisesmayinflateelectricitypricesby
withholding generation capacity to obtain higher profits, but it will
reduce the utilization rate of system electricity, andthe extent to which
their bids deviate from generation costs increases with the degree of
electricity supply scarcity.
Thesecondmechanismispay-as-bid(PAB).ThePABmechanismisto
predicttheelectricitydemandforthenextdayinadvancebythetrading
center under certain constraints, and then the power generation com-
panies declare their own generation capacity and corresponding elec-
tricitypricestothetradingdispatchcenterbasedontheirownsituation
[60]. The dispatch center then sorts the declared electricity prices from
low to high, allocates the generation load to each power generation
company, and conducts transaction settlement based on the actual bids
of each power generation company until the electricity demand for the
next day is met. While maintaining the original bid before and after the
retention capacity, as the retention capacity will not generate higher
profits, in order to achieve optimal production, power generation
companies will report the maximum available capacity, thereby pre-
venting power generation companies from artificially inflating elec-
tricity prices and benefiting from it through the means of retaining
power generation capacity when the system’s power generation capac-
ity is tight. However, compared to the MCP mechanism, it is not suffi-
ciently enriched in economic theory, unable to provide relevant
theoretical guidance for the optimal allocation of resources in the
electricitymarket,andnotconducivetoachievingfaircompetition.This
is because power generators with a large market share will use their
informationadvantagetodominatethecompetition.Inaddition,power
generation companies tend to declare high prices to increase their own

The third is hybrid price settlement mechanism. Under this pricing

In this work of model construction, our focus lies on modeling the
economicinteractionsbetweenrenewableenergygenerationcompanies
(Group (1) and power grid enterprises (Group (2) through an evolu-
tionary game approach, rather than providing a comprehensive power
flowortransientstabilitymodeloftheentiregridsystem.Nonetheless,it
is essential to connect these game-theoretic constructs with real-world
power system constraints. Grid stability, network reliability, and phys-
ical constraints (e.g., transmission capacity, solar PV output variations,
battery energy storage behaviors, and load profiles) are not explicitly
formulatedinourEquations(11)–(19).Instead,theseequationscapture
the economic payoffs and cost structures that stakeholders face in a
market-based bidding environment.
Specifically, Equations (11)–(19) describe how renewable energy
generation companies and power grid enterprises coordinate their in-
terests:(i)theydetailthemarginalcosts,subsidyschemes,andpotential
surplus from renewableenergyintegration; (ii) theyillustrate how both
parties’ evolutionary strategies converge or deviate based on economic
incentives alone.
To extend our model so that it incorporates grid stability and reli-
ability, additional state equations are required to represent real-time
network balance, transmission line limits, and power flow constraints
for solar PV, BESS, or other distributed energy resources. This broader
“co-simulation” approach is generally investigated based on classical
game theory, which couples a conventional game-theoretic structure to
apowersystemsimulator(e.g.,PSS®E,DIgSILENTPowerFactory).Such
integration would enable us to track bus voltages, line flows, contin-
gency analysis, and frequency regulation while maintaining the eco-
nomic decision-making framework.
Nevertheless, one must recognize that our use of evolutionary game
theory (EGT) is aimed primarily at a macro-level bidding decision
problem—one focused on how different groups of players iteratively
adjust their strategies based on aggregate payoffs, rather than on satis-
fying explicit network constraints in real time. EGT fundamentally dif-
fers from classical game-theoretic approaches in several respects: (i) it
assumes bounded rationality and models collective behavior as a
dynamical process in which populations learn or imitate successful
strategiesovermultiplerounds,(ii)itanalyzesthestabilityofstrategies
at a population level (e.g., via evolutionary stable strategies, ESS), and
(iii) it often operates on a longer temporal scale, where the primary
concerniswhethergroupscansustainprofitablestrategiesinthefaceof
ongoing adaptation rather than whether each individual dispatch pre-
cisely meets instantaneous transmission or frequency regulation
constraints.
Because our primary goal is to explore how renewable energy com-
panies and grid enterprises evolve their bidding tactics over extended
periods to maximize economic returns, we abstract away from the
granular details of power flows and operational stability. This abstrac-
tion enables us to focus on the large-scale, long-term outcomes of bid-
dingstrategies—particularlywhetherornotagivenbehaviorcaninvade
and dominate the population under uncertain market conditions. As a
result,the‘fitness’orpayofffunctioninourEGTframeworkemphasizes
profit and cost considerations, rather than, for example, ensuring line
capacities or maintaining voltage magnitudes within safe limits. By
contrast, classical game models that incorporate network constraints
typically handle these aspects through optimization-based or static
equilibrium paradigms under fully rational agents and perfect infor-
mation assumptions.
In future expansions, we intend to bridge the gap between macro-
level payoff-based EGT modeling and the precise physical constraints
of power grids by combining our approach with conventional power
systemsimulators.Thisintegrated,co-simulationdesignwouldallowus
to trace how evolutionary adaptations in bidding strategies ultimately
align (or misalign) with voltage, frequency, and security requirements,
thus creating a more comprehensive tool for policy-making and opera-
tional planning in modern electricity markets.

In previous EGT applications to power markets, single-population

not only captures realistic policy interventions—such as seasonal or
event-driven modifications of bidding caps—but also spotlights emer-

note, such emergentbehaviorsremain understudiedin theexistingEGT
literature;ourresultsaddressthisgapbydemonstratinghowgroup-level
payoffs and strategy proportions oscillate when external constraints
fluctuate repeatedly.
Thus, regardless of the bidding mechanism adopted by the power
generation group, the principle of price priority is emphasized in this
section. For our 2× 2 scenario, we specifically define two categories of
powergenerationcompanies—GroupA(large-scale,low-cost)vs.Group

payoff disparities. In addition, we observe that payoffs and initial con-
ditions significantly shape equilibrium outcomes in the replicator dy-

incrementally adjusting each cost or capacity parameter (±20 %). The
system’s qualitative behavior—i.e., whether cooperation or non-
cooperation dominates—remained consistent, although the speed of

6.2. Conclusions
With the rapid development of market industrialization and the
evolution of power systems with high penetration of distributed power
generation resources, it becomes critical for policymakers and market
operators to consider how changes in generation costs (e.g., driven by
technology breakthroughs or fuel price volatility), local demand pat-
terns (e.g., stimulated by economic growth or shifting consumption
behaviors), and bidding strategies (e.g., via regulatory constraints on
priceintervalsorrewardstructures)jointlyshapethelong-termstability
of the market. Conventional optimization frameworks, although
powerful for certain types of centralized or well-defined decision prob-
lems, lack the necessary structure to handle iterative, multi-agent in-
teractions in a long-term, partially uncertain bidding environment.
Thisstudydemonstratesthattheproposed evolutionarygame-based
model addresses several critical limitations of existing techniques.
Specifically, it overcomes the static assumptions of Nash equilibrium
models [15], the hierarchical constraints of Stackelberg-based ap-
proaches [19], and the oversimplifications in uniform-price auction
frameworks [42]. By incorporating replicator dynamics, the model
captures the dynamic and adaptive behaviors of heterogeneous power
generators, offering a more realistic representation of market
interactions.
However, the proposed method is not without limitations. For
instance, the replicator dynamics framework assumes continuous strat-
egy updates, which may not fully capture the discrete decision-making
processes of market participants. Additionally, the sensitivity of the
model to initial parameter settings highlights the need for robust cali-
bration methods in practical applications. Despite these limitations, the
model provides valuable insights for policymakers, particularly in
designing bidding rules that promote fair competition and prevent
market monopolization
This study presents a novel application of evolutionary game theory
(EGT) to model and analyze the bidding strategies of heterogeneous
power generation companies under the market-clearing price (MCP)
mechanism. Unlike traditional static models, the proposed framework
captures the dynamic, bounded rational behaviors of market partici-
pants, reflecting real-world complexities. The integration of EGT with
the MCP mechanism provides a unique lens to explore the evolution of
bidding strategies, offering insights into achieving equilibrium states
that align with market efficiency and policy goals. Key findings include
the critical role of regulatory measures in promoting fair and competi-
tive bidding, the importance of aligning strategies with marginal costs,
and the potential to lower overall electricity prices while supporting
renewable energy integration.
This study not only advances the theoretical understanding of bid-
ding behaviors but also provides practical implications for designing
effective market regulations and fostering sustainable energy systems.
Future research can build on this framework to explore additional var-
iablesandexpanditsapplicabilitytodiversemarketscenarios,ensuring
its relevance in the evolving landscape of global energy markets.
The evolutionary game framework we propose offers an insightful
lens for policymakers seeking to nudge stakeholders toward mutually
beneficial bidding behaviors. By observing the replicator dynamics
amongrenewableenergycompaniesandgridenterprises,regulatorscan
identify which incentive structures (e.g., feed-in tariffs, carbon credits)
effectively drive long-term cooperation, while also recognizing the
conditions under which defection or free-riding might proliferate.
