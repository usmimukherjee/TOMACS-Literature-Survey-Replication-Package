Abstract. In the paper we show how L-FABS can be applied in a partial knowledge learning scenario or a full knowledge learning
scenario to approximate ﬁnancial time series. L-FABS combines agent-based simulation with machine learning to model the
behavior of ﬁnancial time series.
We also discuss why Partial Knowledge and Full Knowledge learning scenario are relevant to the modeling of ﬁnancial time
series and how they can be used to assess the robustness of a modeling system for ﬁnancial time series. In a Partial Knowledge
learning setting usually only the initial conditions of the time series are provided, while in a Full Knowledge learning scenario
any value of the ﬁnancial time series is exploited as soon as it is available.
An extensive experimental analysis of L-FABS is reported under a variety of ﬁnancial time series and time frames.
Keywords: Agent-based modeling and simulation, partial knowledge learning, full knowledge learning, simulated annealing,
ﬁnancial markets, prediction of SP500, DJIA, Google, SPY time series, 1 minute, 10 minutes, 1 hour, daily time series
1. Introduction
Financial markets can be viewed as complex sys-
tems whose basic entities and interactions can be easily
described. However, when ﬁnancial markets are taken
into account as a whole, no theory or empirical method
is able to explain or predict with certainty what will
happen during their future evolution. Indeed the pros
and cons of using quantitative methodologies for their
studies are still open topic for the scientiﬁc debate [7,
12].
The state-of-the-art literature shows that agent-based
modeling (ABM) is a ﬂexible modeling methodology
for simulating several types of domains, when inter-
preted as complex systems, like, for instance, con-
sumer markets, economies or societies [2,4,5,10,11,
20,29]. Thus ABM could be a promising candidate
for investigating ﬁnancial markets. Current research on
modeling ﬁnancial markets usually focuses on: evalu-
ating or learning trading strategies for ﬁnancial instru-
ments (commodities, bonds, shares, etc.) [9,15,27]; or
developing artiﬁcial markets, whose internal dynamics
can be controlled, in order to study the formation of no-
table phenomena (e.g., price bubble) [1,14,28]; or, ﬁ-
nally, modeling the time series of the values/prices for
ﬁnancial assets [18,23].1
By considering the structure of ﬁnancial markets, it
can be observed that the market as a whole is made up
of the investment decisions of many individuals, the in-
vestors. If we try and match this structural perspective
about ﬁnancial markets with the observations made by
ABM researchers, we open up the possibility of study-
ing ﬁnancial markets by using computational simu-
lation. For researchers active in the software agents
community, computational simulation takes the form
of agent-based simulators where hypothesis about the
decision making process of the individual and about
the relationships occurring among them could be tested
[3,10,14,19,29]. One of the main difﬁculties encoun-
tered by these works stays in tuning the model so that
the simulated behavior approximate the observed one.
With this respect machine learning algorithms, and
simulated annealing [17] in the case of our research,
can be used to learn the main parameters governing the
simulation process as discussed in [23].

The research reported in this work originates from

(2) the description of how L-FABS can operate under

(3) the study of the behavior of L-FABS over differ-

evaluate its robustness under several experimen-

two time series

for a time series, we need to select some error function
to be minimized during learning that is able to eval-
uate how well two time series (the estimated and the
target one) are similar. The Mean Squared Error and
the Mean Absolute Percentage Error fulﬁll our require-
ments and are commonly used in Statistics when two
data samplings have to be compared. They are deﬁned

Given two time series X and Y , the lower the values
for MSE and MAPE, the closer the two are. MSE pro-
vides an absolute measure for the error while MAPE
returns the error measured in percentage terms. The
squared root of MSE is known as the Standard Error.
The MSE or Standard Error are useful error measures
when comparing the approximation error between sev-
eral estimated time series for the same target one: as
the time series have the same data range, a measure of
error in absolute terms makes sense. Instead the MAPE
is more suitable when comparing the approximation
error for time series having different data ranges. In
this case an error expressed as a percentage is more
suitable in evaluating their degree of similarity. As in
our work, we will compare time series having different
value ranges, the MAPE has been chosen to measure
the approximation error of our system.
3. Partial Knowledge learning vs. Full Knowledge
learning
In this section, we explain the difference between
learning scenarios where partial or complete knowl-
edge of the data points in the time series is available.
This information will facilitate the understanding of
the experimental setups reported in the following. For
machine learning researchers, the distinctions between
partial knowledge learning and full knowledge learn-
ing can recall the categorization of learning systems
in as being able to operate when no additional domain
knowledge is available, apart from the data, or when
additional domain theory, maybe even a causal model
of the domain, is available [26].
In our study, we consider the effect of having access
or not having access to the real values of a time series
from time 0 up to the current time t when forecasting
its future values from time t + 1 and ahead. In gen-
eral, when making a prediction, all learning systems
try to exploit every bit of information in order to re-

duce their forecasting error. This is a common and rea-
sonable approach when employing a machine learning
system. However, it can also be noted that, when mod-
eling a time series, the use of all the information up
to the current time t is generally a necessity to obtain
an acceptable forecasting error as the majority of the
learning systems base their predictions on the identiﬁ-
cation of patterns in the recent part of the target time
series [8]. Thus it follows that most (if not all) pattern
learning methods operate under Full Knowledge learn-
ing scenarios.
In our research, we will name Full Knowledge sce-
nario (or FK) a learning set up where all the target
time series data points up to the current time are avail-
able to the learner, while we name Partial Knowledge
scenario (PK) the learning set up where only the ini-
tial data point of the target time series is available to
the learner. Most machine learning systems operating
through pattern matching will not be able to work in
a PK learning scenario with acceptable approximation
errors. L-FABS, instead, bases its predictions on the
simulation of the investment process that underlies the
market thus L-FABS can either take advantage of all
the data in the time series or it can just use the initial
conditions of the market, data at time zero while main-
taining acceptable approximation errors. L-FABS can
operate both in a PK or FK learning scenario.
As the PK scenario exploits less information than
the FK one, it corresponds to a more difﬁcult learn-
ing/approximation task. We are interested in the PK
scenario because it can provide insights about the “ro-
bustness” of the model acquired by L-FABS. By ro-
bustness we mean the feature of the model to have in-
trinsically captured the law of evolution in time of the
time series which can be measured by the maintenance
of accuracy in forecasts as the system move far from
the point of origin.
4. Empirical analysis
For the empirical evaluation of L-FABS, we consid-
ered a few ﬁnancial time series to work with. As usual
with learning systems, we will train L-FABS on a part
of the dataset, the learning set, and then we will use
the remaining part of the dataset as test set to assess
the performances of the learned model. We will present
two distinct groups of experiments that differ for the
considered ﬁnancial time series (SP500, DJIA, Google
and SPY) and for their time frame (1 min, 10 min, 1 h,
daily). The ﬁrst group of experiments will be done on a
daily time frame, while the second set of experiments
will be done on shorter time frames (intra daily). The
objective is to support any empirical ﬁndings by eval-
uating L-FABS on a wide range of operating condi-
tions.

6. Conclusions
In the paper, we have reported an extensive empir-
ical study about the approximation of real ﬁnancial
time series by using our system L-FABS that exploits
agent-based simulations together with machine learn-
ing. L-FABS has been operated under a PK learning
scenario and a FK learning one. The main results re-
ported in this work are:
(1) the description of what a PK learning scenario
and a FK one are;
(2) the analysis of the behavior of L-FABS over dif-
ferent time series, time frames, distances in time
of the target value, that shows the robustness of
L-FABS under several experimental settings;
(3) we also believe that our ﬁndings support the
working hypothesis that agent-based simulations
together with learning algorithms are particularly
suitable for approximating real ﬁnancial time se-
ries because of their ability to model the struc-
tures of the processes generating the ﬁnancial
time series.
Appendix A: The simple ﬁnancial agent-based
simulator
L-FABS [23] is made of two interacting compo-
nents: the Simple Financial Agent Based Simulator,
S-FABS, which implement the agent-based simulator
and a learning algorithm, Simulated Annealing in our
case. In this section, we describe the agent-based sim-
ulator S-FABS.
The abstraction of ﬁnancial markets that we used to
design L-FABS takes into account that the behavior of
the investors is driven by two main factors:
(a) the propensity to take some investment risks to-
day, by buying a ﬁnancial asset, in exchange for
a future uncertain reward, when selling the as-
set; and
(b) the common consensus (the market sentiment or
just sentiment) about the future behavior of the
market itself. If the people believe that the eco-
nomic outlook will be negative, each individual
will tend to sell some of her/his assets.
We can now start describing the main component
of our simulator: the FinancialAgent. A Financial-
Agent implements the decision making process un-
derlying the investment decision of buying, selling
or holding an asset. In details, a FinancialAgent will
decide to buy some asset with probability P( X<
BuyThreshold), or it will hold to its assets with prob-
ability P(BuyThreshold <X< SellThreshold), or
it will sell some of its assets with probability P( X>
SellThreshold). The abstract algorithm for a Finan-
cialAgent is appearing in Algorithm 1.
As it can be seen in the algorithm, the probabilities
to buy/sell/hold are dependent on the current level of
sentiment about the economy. In fact the probability
to buy assets increases along with the sentiment, while
the probability to sell assets decreases when the sen-
timent is raising and vice versa. Also the amount of
assets to be bought or sold is set to the average per-
centual variation over the target time frame (AvgTF-
Var) of the time series (as measured over the learning
set). We veriﬁed empirically since an earlier work [22]
that this value provides reasonable simulation results
and the function has been kept constant since then.

of the entire ﬁnancial market can be obtained by imple-
menting the system architecture described by the algo-
rithm reported in Algorithm 2 thus realizing the Simple
Financial Agent Based Simulator S-FABS.

In S-FABS, several instances of FinancialAgent are
created, each one with its own status in terms of own
assets, invested assets and risk/reward propensity. Then
an investment round is performed during which each
agent decides if buying, selling, or holding taking into
account the Sentiment value for the round. At the end
of the round, it is possible to measure the percentage
of invested assets. This percentage is used as an esti-
mate for one data point of the target time series. If the
investment round is repeated n times, the output will
represent an estimate for ann-length time series.
S-FABS takes as input the vector of risk/rewards
propensity rates for each Financial Agent. During each
round, the risk/rewards propensity rates are used in
combination with the current value of the economic
sentiment by each Financial Agent to take an invest-
ment decision.
For our research we implemented the Sentiment
function as shown below:
function Sentiment(time, mavgDays)
begin = time − 1; end = time-mavgDays
BearishSentiment = {learned value}
BullishSentiment = {learned value}
if (MA VG(PredictedData, begin, end)<
MA VG(RealData, begin, end))
then return(BullishSentiment)
else return(BearishSentiment)
The variables RealData and PredictedData give ac-
cess to the time series of the real and predicted val-
ues. According to our choice for the Sentiment func-
tion [23], the outlook of the real market is considered
bullish if the moving average 3 (MA VG) of the pre-
dicted time series is lower than the moving average of
the real time series. If this is the case, the Sentiment
value is set to an high value so that a bullish mood is
communicated to the agents. The opposite happens if
the predictions of the system have been higher than the
real data.

Appendix B: The complete learning simulator
L-FABS: Adding a learning capability to S-FABS
In this section, we describe how a learning capa-
bility can be added to S-FABS so that it may ﬁnd
the best model for a given time series. The resulting
system will be called L-FABS. Learning in L-FABS
consists of ﬁnding the vector of risk/reward propen-
sity rates plus the BullishSentiment and BearishSen-
timent values that approximates a given time series
with a minimum error. This learning setting allows
for combining S-FABS, the simulation engine, with
any of the many machine learning algorithms able to
ﬁnd a vector of values that minimizes a given error
function. Examples of suitable machine learning al-
gorithms include genetic algorithms [13,21], decision
trees [24], neural networks [25], simulated annealing
[17] and many others. In our study, we decided to use
simulated annealing because evolutionary algorithms
proved to be robust and well performing across several
domains [13,17] and an individual oriented method is
less computationally expensive than a population ori-
ented one.
Simulated annealing [17] is a searching algorithm
that iteratively searches for the minimum of a given
function E (the Energy or Error function) by gen-
erating at each step random candidate points for the
minimum in the proximity of the current candidate
minimum point which represents the “center” of the
exploration. At the end of each step, Simulated An-
nealing may move the center of its exploration at a
newly generated point with a given probability. Run-
ning L-FABS consists of running Simulated Annealing
to search the vector space of the risk/reward propen-
sity rates in order to ﬁnd one that minimizes the er-
ror function. At each round of annealing, the candidate
vector,s, is used as input to S-FABS. S-FABS then re-
turns an estimated time series to approximate the given
targetTimeSeries. The approximation error measured
by MAPE is used to decide if the candidate vector is an
improved solution. The template for L-FABS is shown
in Algorithm 3.
