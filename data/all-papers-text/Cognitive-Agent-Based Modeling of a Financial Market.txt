Abstract
This paper describes our experience in building an evo-
lutionary system for agent-based modeling of a ﬁnancial
market. The system uses a type of BDI agents, which
are deliberative agents with a mental state deﬁned by a
belief base and by a set of desire-generation rules. Beliefs
are graded and trust in information sources is taken into
account. At any moment, an agent generates a set of desires
and selects a consistent subset thereof, whose elements are
adopted as goals, to be achieved by executing actions.
The market structure is realistic, albeit simpliﬁed: a single
asset is assumed to exist, that is traded on the market in
indistinguishable, standardized contracts against payment of
money. The price of trades is continuously determined by
means of a single-price auction, which crosses buy and sell
orders to maximize market liquidity. Every agent has, at any
moment, an available amount of money and an inventory of
asset contracts, which, valued at market price and added to
the available money, yields the agent’s net asset value (NA V).
To determine their economical behavior , the agents have
access to a set of technical indicators made available by
the market, which constitute the sources of their beliefs, and
to their current balance, which constitutes their knowledge.
The agents participating in the market evolve by means of
an evolutionary algorithm: they undergo selection based on
their NA V , replication with random mutations, and recombi-
nation. When an agent replicates, its properties are divided
among the offspring. This evolutionary process favors the
reproduction of proﬁtable traders, allowing the emergence
of “sensible” behaviors.
The market thus simulated has been analyzed both in
quantitative and in qualitative terms. The analysis has
demonstrated that the model exhibits several traits that are
typical of real-world ﬁnancial markets.
1. Introduction
In the last decade, agent-based modeling has become a
widely accepted tool for studying the dynamics of ﬁnancial
markets [1]. Agent-based modeling is the computational
study, carried out by means of simulation, of economies
that are modeled as evolving systems of autonomous in-
teracting agents [2]. The main advantage of agent-based
over analytical models is that they allow the removal of
restrictive assumptions and can, thus, lead to a more realistic
description of the system under investigation.
A prototypical and very inﬂuential example of an agent-
based system of this kind is the Santa Fe Institute Artiﬁcial
Stock Market (SFI-ASM) [3]. The traders in the SFI-ASM
have the same utility function; at each period, they have
to decide how much to invest in risky stock, which pays a
stochastic dividend, and how much to keep in cash, which
yields a risk-free return. Traders differ from one another
in the way they compute their expectations about future
prices and dividends from the same information set by
means of sets of rules that are a modiﬁed version of John
Holland’s classiﬁer system [4]. Each agent uses its own
genetic algorithm (GA) to alter its rules by replacing poorly
performing rules with new, possibly better ones. The SFI-
ASM is able to reproduce many of the stylized facts of real-
world ﬁnancial markets [1].
While usually agent-based models of economies rely on
very simple agents, we thought interesting to investigate
the use of richer, more sophisticated agent types, that more
closely reproduce the characters of the participants in real-
world economies, namely people. A distinctive character of
human participants in the ﬁnancial markets is their having
cognitive capabilities, and their deciding their behaviour
through rational deliberation. An agent model that strives
to capture these aspects is the BDI (belief, desire, intention)
model [5].
In this work, we consider beliefs, desires, and goals, in
addition, following [6], we allow an agent to believe a piece
of information to a degree. Such degree depends on the trust
the agent has in the source of information. At any moment,
an agent generates a set of desires and selects a consistent
subset thereof, whose elements are adopted as goals, to be
achieved by executing suitable actions.
A fundamental idea which underlies our approach to
agent-based modeling is to let the agents participating in
the market evolve under the pressure of competition-driven
selection: proﬁtable agents proliferate, while unproﬁtable
agents become extinct.

2. Market Structure
The market structure is realistic, albeit simpliﬁed: a single
asset is assumed to exist, that is traded on the market as
a commodity, in indistinguishable, standardized contracts
against the payment of money. We make the simplifying
assumption that the asset does not pay dividends and the
money does not earn interests. Likewise, we assume no
transaction fees are claimed by the market and no expenses
are payable for the storage and transportation of the asset.
To participate in the market, the agents must enter buy
or sell orders specifying the quantity (number of contracts)
and the limit price (per contract), including 0 and +∞,s o
that “market orders” can be simulated.
Differently, in the SFI-ASM there are two kinds of in-
vestment: free-risk stocks, comparable to real-world bonds,
and risky stocks. The risky stocks are in ﬁnite supply.
Furthermore, they pay a stochastic dividend that changes
over time and whose current value is unknown to the agents.
Also the low-risk assets, comparable to our money, pay a
constant interest rate [7].
The orders entered by the participants are matched by
the market by executing a single-price auction every time
a new order is received. This is very realistic, in that a
single-price auction underlies the structure of practically
all real-world regulated ﬁnancial markets, whose rules may
differ in subtle details of how special cases are handled, or
how continuous negotiation is started, stopped, halted, and
resumed. Single-price auctions determine the actual price
of trades in a way that maximizes the number of contracts
traded and, therefore, market liquidity.
Rather than negotiating the price through an auction,
in the SFI-ASM there is a particular agent, called the
“specialist,” which initializes the price formation process by
setting a “trial price,” equal to last period’s price. Based on
this price, the agents submit their bids and offers. If they
do not match, the specialist announces a new trial price and
the process goes on iteratively as long as the number of bids
and offers do not exactly match [1].
Every agent has, at any moment, an available amount of
money and an inventory of asset contracts, which, valued
at market price and added to the available money, yields
the agent’s net asset value (NA V). An agent’s NA V is the
primary indicator of its performance.
The agents operate in a regime of incomplete information,
in that every agent knows its current money balance and
asset inventory, as well as the orders it submits, but cannot
directly observe neither the orders entered by the other
market participants, nor the balance and inventory of its
peers. Instead, to determine their economical behavior, the
agents have access to a set of technical indicators made
available by the market, which constitute the only sources
of their beliefs.
2.1. Market Simulator
The market simulator is inspired to the Environment-
Rules-Agents (ERA) scheme [8], whereby the model con-
sists of four layers:
1) the environment layer, where the agents interact — in
our case, this is the market;
2) the agents layer;
3) the rules layer, where the rules that deﬁne the bahavior
of agents are represented and executed;
4) the rule-construction layer, where the algorithms that
allow agent behavior to evolve or adapt are deﬁned
— in our case, this is the evolutionary algorithm
described in Section 4.
Practically all ﬁnancial markets are order-driven: partici-
pants express their willingness to buy or sell a security or a
commodity at a given price by placing an order. An offer to
buy at a given price or less is called a bid; an offer to sell
at a given price or more is called an ask. Bids and asks for
the same security or commodity are usually placed on two
“books”, ranked from the best (or, in case of tie, earliest) to
the worst (or, in case of tie, latest). Then, matching orders
in the books are executed according to that ranking [9].
The market simulator essentially receives orders from the
participating agents, organizes them into the bid and ask
books, matches them by means of a single-price auction, and
clears the trades by transferring the appropriate amounts of
money and asset among the agents. Furthermore, the market
simulator saves the market clearing price for each period
into a log, and uses the logged prices to evaluate a number
of technical indicators, which are “published”, i.e., made
available to all participating agents.
The above cycle, called a period, which is the clock that
measures time in a simulation, is repeated over and over. We
will denote by t the current period in a simulation. Every
given number of periods, which is a fundamental parameter
of a simulation, a generation of the evolutionary algorithm
is performed.

2.3. Technical Indicators
A technical indicator is a short-term statistics of a time
series used by technical analysts to predict future price
movements of ﬁnancial instruments.
The available technical indicators, summarized in Table 1,
are divided into ﬁve categories:
1–5) variation of prices;
6–11) comparison between prices and mobile averages;
12–15) comparison between the ratios of price to fast and
price to slow mobile averages;
16–21) variation of the ratios of price to a mobile average;
22) the MACD technical indicator.
A moving average is the average of the prices of a
ﬁnancial instrument over a given time period. Generally
speaking, moving averages tend to smooth out the short-term
oscillations of a time series and identify longer-term trends.
The two most popular types of moving average indicators
are the simple moving average ( SMA) and the exponential
moving average ( EMA). The difference between these in-
dicators is uniquely on the weight each of them carry to
recent prices compared to old prices. SMA considers equally
important all the prices in a considered period, whereas
EMA considers that recent prices are more important than
the old ones. In general, a buy signal is generated when the
instrument’s price rises above a moving average and a sell
signal is generated when the instrument’s price falls below
a moving average.

3. The Agents
The participants in the market are modeled as cognitive
agents, with a mental state consisting of beliefs, knowledge,
and desires, whose behavior is governed by a deliberative
process that generates goals from beliefs, knowledge, and
desires.
Furthermore, the agents are evolutionary, in the sense that
they are individuals of an evolutionary algorithm, whose
details are illustrated in Section 4. Therefore, every agent
has a genome, which determines what might be called its
“instincts”, in practice a set of parameters that inﬂuence the
way the agent reacts to stimuli from the environment, i.e., the
market. These parameters, which are genetically determined,
do not change during the agent’s entire lifetime, whereas the
agent’s mental state may change in response to changes in
the market and in the agent’s ﬁnancial conditions.
The cognitive agent model is essentially the one discussed
in [10]. However, besides explicitly considering the agent’s
beliefs, desires, and goals like in [10], we also explicitly
consider the agent’s knowledge. Here, as usual [11], an
agent’s knowledge refers to information it has direct access
to, whereas beliefs refer to anything the agent cannot directly
observe, like, in our case, future states of affairs.
Therefore, an agent has knowledge of its balance and
inventory, as well as of the orders it sent to the market;
likewise, an agent has beliefs on the future movements of
the price of the asset, i.e., it can be bearish, if it believes
prices are going down, or bullish, if it believes prices are
going up.
An agent’s desire can be usefully regarded as a candidate
goal, i.e., a motivation that must meet other conditions to
become a goal.
The mental state of agents changes when their knowledge
changes, which happens when an order is executed and/or
the market sets a new price for the asset, or when their
beliefs change, based on a number of technical indicators
made available by the market.
Knowledge, beliefs, desires, and goals are represented by
means of fuzzy propositions, i.e., propositions whose degree
of truth can take any value between 0 and 1.
3.1. Fuzzy Propositions
Fuzzy sets, introduced by Zadeh [12], are a generalization
of classical sets obtained by replacing the characteristic
function of a set A with a membership function μA, which
can take up any value in [0, 1]. The value μA(x) or, more
simply, A(x) is the membership degree of element x in A,
i.e., the degree to which x belongs in A. The support of A,
supp(A), is the set of all x such that A(x) > 0.
The usual set-theoretic operations of union, intersection,
and complement can be deﬁned as a generalization of their
counterparts on classical sets by introducing two families of
operators, called triangular norms and co-norms. In practice,
it is usual to employ the min norm for intersection and the
max co-norm for union. Given two fuzzy sets A and B,
and an element x, (A ∪ B)(x) = max {A(x),B (x)}, (A ∩
B)(x) = min {A(x),B (x)}, and ¯A(x)=1 − A(x).
Deﬁnition 1 (Fuzzy Interpretation): A fuzzy interpreta-
tion is an assignment of truth degrees in [0, 1] to all atomic
propositions (or atoms, for short) deﬁned in the problem
domain. Given a set of atoms A, a fuzzy interpretation is
a function I : A→ [0, 1], which assigns a truth degree
I(p) ∈ [0, 1] to all atoms p ∈A .
Note that a fuzzy interpretation is, in all respects, a fuzzy
set of atoms.

goals. The main distinction we made here between desires

completely constrained genetically would defeat the purpose
of adopting a cognitive model for agents.

3.5. Comparison with SFI-ASM Agents
In the SFI-ASM, the agents take their investment de-
cisions by using a pool of market forecasting rules that
determine the value of some variables involved in the linear
forecast computation of next period’s price and dividend.
The market forecasting rules follow this model: IF market
meets certain conditions THEN variables have certain con-
stant values. Agents can use different rules, but those rules
are static. Differently, in our simulator, every agent uses
the same rules, but the parameters of those functions are
different for every agent [7].
The evolutionary algorithm focuses on different aspects in
the two simulators: in the SFI-ASM, the whole rule pools
are involved, while in our simulator only the parameters are
evolved by the algorithm.
A last, but important, difference between the two models
involves the ﬁtness function. In the SFI-ASM, the ﬁtness
calculation is based on the accuracy and complexity of the
rule pools. In our simulator, ﬁtness is based on the NA V of
an agent. In other words, we select trading rules that have
led the agent to gain more wealth than its peers, while the
SFI-ASM selects predictive rules considered successful a
priori.
4. Evolutionary Algorithm
EAs [13], [14] are a broad class of stochastic optimization
algorithms, inspired by biology and in particular by those
biological processes that allow populations of organisms to
adapt to their surrounding environment: genetic inheritance
and survival of the ﬁttest. Each individual of the population
represents a point in the space of the potential solutions
for the considered problem. The evolution is obtained by
iteratively applying a (usually quite small) set of stochastic
operators, known as mutation, recombination, and selection.
Mutation randomly perturbs a candidate solution; recombi-
nation decomposes two distinct solutions and then randomly
mixes their parts to form novel solutions; and selection repli-
cates the most successful solutions found in a population
at a rate proportional to their relative quality. The initial
population may be either a random sample of the solution
space or may be seeded with solutions found by simple
local search procedures, if these are available. The resulting
process tends to ﬁnd, given enough time, globally optimal

solutions to the problem much in the same way as in nature

An evolutionary algorithm is used by the simulator to
make the agents participating in the market evolve according
to their trading proﬁciency.
The evolutionary algorithm proceeds concurrently with
the trading activity in the market. A generation is performed
at regular intervals, whose length, i, measured in trading
periods, is a parameter of the simulation.
4.1. Representation
The parameters listed in Table 2, which make up the
genome of an agent, are encoded as an array of 33 ﬁxed-
point numbers.
4.2. Initialization
The genomes of the agents in the initial population are
generated at random. However, every agent is constructed so
that it takes only 11 of the 22 available technical indicators
into account.
4.3. Fitness and Selection
The ﬁtness of an agent is given by its NA V . At each
generation, the 30% of the individuals having the lowest
NA V is eliminated from the simulation. The remaining 70%
is given a chance of reproducing.
The properties (money and asset inventory) of the agents
that become extinct are distributed to the surviving agents,
proportionately to their NA V . This guarantees that the total
stock of money and of the asset are constant. Furthermore,
being NA V -proportionate, the redistribution of properties
does not bias the relative wealths of the surviving agents.
The surviving agents are selected for reproduction with a
probability proportional to their NA V . Selection is elitist, in
that the agent with the highest NA V is always selected at
least once.
4.4. Reproduction
As many new agents are generated by reproduction as
became extinct, so that the population size remains constant
over the generations.
First of all, a clone of the agent with the highest NA V
is inserted into the population. Then, until the population is
complete, two of the surviving agents are selected and a new
agent is created from them by recombination and mutation.
Recombination is performed by uniform crossover,
whereas mutation applies a Gaussian perturbation (with
mean 0 and standard deviation 0.01) to every gene of an
agent’s genome.
The new agent thus generated inherits 25% of the prop-
erties of both parents. Therefore, the NA V of both parents,
and their probability to be selected, decreases by 25% for
each agent they generate. This, once again, guarantees that
the total stock of money and of the asset remains constant,
while favoring the reproduction of wealthy (and proﬁcient)
agents.
5. Experiments and Results
Three simulation scenarios have been investigated, which
differ by the number i (i ∈{ 0, 10, 100}) of trading periods
between two successive generations of the evolutionary
algorithm. All simulations have been carried out with a
population of 100 agents.
Figure 1 shows typical price dynamics in the three simu-
lation scenarios. We have observed a consistent pattern over
the simulations that have been carried out: prices seem to
follow a random walk, but their series is little volatile and
mean-reverting when evolution acts continuously ( i =1 );
as more trading periods are allowed between generations
(i =1 0 ), price becomes very unstable and bubbles and
severe crashes appear; if an even longer interval between
generations is allowed (i = 100), the behavior of the market
is similar, qualitatively, but bubbles and crashes become
more frequent and less severe. V olatility too decreases:
whilst the variance of price is around 600 for i =1 0 ,i t
dives to around 125 for i = 100.
By looking at the simulation logs, we discovered that
crashes usually occur in the ﬁrst trading period after a gener-
ation of the evolutionary algorithm has been performed. At
those moments, the composition of the population of agents
changes substantially, when below-average agents are wiped
out by selection and replaced by the offspring of above-
average agents; all of a sudden, the collective behavior of
the traders has changed, and the market must ﬁnd a new
equilibrium. A similar complexity regime has been observed
in the SFI-ASM as well [1] with fast learning. Like in
the SFI-ASM, when evolution proceeds more slowly, this
phenomenon tends to get damped.
The distribution of log-returns of the time series of asset
prices is leptokurtic:
• for i =1 , excess kurtosis is 44;
• for i =1 0, excess kurtosis is 1.28;
• for i = 100, excess kurtosis is 7.44.
Figure 2 shows that the ﬁnal distribution of wealth (money
+ asset inventory) in a population depends on the interval
i between generations. The tendence is for the wealth to
become less equally distributed for larger intervals between
generations.

6. Conclusion
The simulated market exhibits the key stylized facts of
real ﬁnancial markets, such as volatility clustering, leptokur-
tosis of log-returns, bubbles and crashes, and mean-reverting
time series.
The main difference between our approach to agent-based
modeling and the SFI-ASM is that in the SFI-ASM, a
GA is used by each agent participating in the market as a
learning algorithm. Therefore, there are as many independent
GAs as agents in the system. In our approach, instead,
individual agents don’t learn, but the population of all agents
participating in the market is subjected to an evolutionary
algorithms and, thus, evolves as a whole. Since agents are
evaluated based on their proﬁciency at trading with their
peers, what we have here is a kind of competitive co-
evolution, which is typical of artiﬁcial life.
A limitation of our approach in the current version is that
the desire-generation rules of the agents can be just one
of eight possible pre-deﬁned sets. A natural extension for
future work would be to let evolution ﬁgure out the set of
desire-generation rules without any constraints.
